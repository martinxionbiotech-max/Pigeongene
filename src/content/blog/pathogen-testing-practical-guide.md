Every racing season, fanciers face the same question: is my loft healthy enough to race? Clinical signs alone rarely give the answer, because the early stages of many pigeon diseases are easy to miss. This guide explains when testing helps, what a laboratory can actually detect, and how to interpret the results honestly.

## Why test before you see symptoms?

In many infections, birds shed pathogens before visible signs appear. A bird that looks fine can still transmit pathogens to the rest of the team during transport and basketing. Regular pre-season screening and quarantine testing catch problems earlier and reduce the risk of spread — which is the practical value of proactive testing.

## What can a panel cover?

A typical racing-pigeon panel covers 12 common pathogens across four categories:

- **Viruses (7)**: Pigeon Poxvirus, Pigeon Polyomavirus, Pigeon Rotavirus, Pigeon Herpesvirus, Pigeon Adenovirus, Pigeon Circovirus, Newcastle Disease Virus
- **Bacteria (3)**: Salmonella spp., Chlamydia psittaci, Mycoplasma spp.
- **Fungus (1)**: Candida albicans
- **Parasite (1)**: Trichomonas gallinae

Detection is performed by real-time fluorescent PCR (qPCR), the accepted reference method for pathogen nucleic-acid detection. The method targets specific genetic sequences of each pathogen, so a panel test gives per-pathogen results rather than a single "sick or healthy" answer.

## When should you test?

Three situations justify testing most often:

1. **Pre-season screening** — establish a health baseline before racing or breeding begins.
2. **Quarantine intake** — test new birds before they join the loft.
3. **Outbreak investigation** — when symptoms appear, identify the cause before treating.

Respiratory and digestive symptoms overlap across many pathogens, so when several birds show signs, a combined panel is usually more informative than a single-pathogen test.

## How sampling works

DNA testing accepts three sample types, each with its own trade-off:

- **Feathers with intact calamus** (3–5) — easy to collect, non-invasive
- **Blood cards** — suitable when feather quality is poor
- **Oral swabs** — preferred for respiratory and upper-digestive pathogens

Samples should be labelled, sealed, and shipped with the submission form. A full sampling guide is available on the main site.

## Reading the report honestly

A report lists each pathogen as detected, not detected, or weak-positive. Three points prevent misinterpretation:

- **A negative result reflects the sample at the time of collection.** Sampling timing, sample quality and pathogen load all affect detection. A single negative does not prove a bird is healthy.
- **Symptoms alone cannot confirm a diagnosis.** Laboratory detection is the basis; treatment decisions should follow veterinary advice.
- **Weak positives deserve follow-up.** Low pathogen load can mean early infection or a carrier state.

Routine panels take 2–3 working days after the sample arrives, depending on the project and sample receipt time.

## Bottom line

Testing works best as part of a routine: pre-season screening, quarantine intake, and targeted investigation when problems appear. Combine laboratory results with clinical observation and loft management — no single test replaces good husbandry.
