Adenovirus is one of the common pathogens of racing pigeons, and it flares up especially after stress such as basketing and transport, often causing what fanciers call "crop disease" or "vomiting disease." Distinguishing the different types of adenovirus correctly is the first step toward a science-based response.

## Types of Pigeon Adenovirus

Pigeon adenovirus (PiADV) is divided mainly into two serotypes:

- **Type I (PiADV-I, also called classical adenovirus)**: causes mainly **gastrointestinal signs** and is the most common adenovirus disease of racing pigeons; typical signs are vomiting, watery diarrhea and depression, with cases especially common after basketing during the racing season.
- **Type II (PiADV-II)**: attacks mainly the **liver**, and can cause hepatomegaly and hepatic necrosis; the disease is more severe but relatively uncommon.

What fanciers usually call "adenovirus disease" mostly refers to the gastrointestinal form caused by Type I. Telling the two apart requires laboratory testing; signs alone are not enough.

## Typical Signs

**Note**: the signs below **cannot be used for diagnosis on their own**. Several diseases present in a similar way, and laboratory testing is needed for confirmation.

- Vomiting, bringing up undigested feed;
- Watery or pasty diarrhea;
- Depression, ruffled feathers, reduced appetite;
- Feed or gas retained in the crop;
- Type II may be accompanied by an enlarged liver and jaundice-like signs.

## Diagnostic Methods

Confirming adenovirus depends on laboratory testing:

1. **Real-time fluorescent PCR (qPCR)**: cloacal swabs or feces are collected and viral nucleic acid is detected; this is a rapid and sensitive confirmatory method;
2. **Epidemiological history**: whether there has been recent stress such as basketing, transport or mixing of groups;
3. **Differential diagnosis**: it must be distinguished from Newcastle disease, rotavirus infection and others.

## Management and Control

There is currently no specific antiviral drug for adenovirus disease; management relies mainly on supportive care and biosecurity:

- **Isolate sick birds**: prevent the virus from spreading within the loft;
- **Fluids and nutritional support**: address the dehydration caused by vomiting and diarrhea;
- **Reduce stress**: pay attention to husbandry before and after basketing during the racing season;
- **Control secondary infection**: use medication rationally under veterinary guidance.

## Key Prevention Points

The key to preventing adenovirus is **lowering stress + biosecurity**:

1. Strengthen nutrition and supplement electrolytes before and after basketing and transport;
2. Isolate and observe newly introduced birds;
3. Disinfect the environment and drinkers regularly;
4. Step up monitoring during high-risk periods (the racing season) so problems are found and isolated early.

## Case in Practice: The "Vomiting Storm" After Basketing

**Scenario**: after returning from the first basketing of the autumn season, on day 3 six birds in the loft developed vomiting and watery droppings one after another, and one of them threw up undigested feed.

**How it was worked up**:

1. **Isolate first**: birds with signs were caged separately at once, and vomit and droppings were cleaned up and disinfected promptly to block fecal-oral transmission;
2. **Review the stress history**: basketing, mixing in crates and transport are high-risk settings for adenovirus, and the timeline fitted closely;
3. **Submit samples for confirmation**: cloacal swabs were taken for qPCR to confirm whether adenovirus was involved and to rule out pathogens with similar signs such as Salmonella;
4. **Manage the signs**: after Type I was confirmed, the focus was on **rehydration to prevent dehydration, withdrawing hard-to-digest feed and reducing the burden on the crop** rather than reaching for drugs blindly;
5. **Review prevention**: cutting back high-fat, high-protein feeding before basketing and observing birds in separate cages for 2-3 days after they return is an easier strategy than treatment.

**Lesson**: similar signs do not mean the same cause — a "vomiting disease" may be adenovirus, or it may be Salmonella or a husbandry problem. A test report is what makes medication and management targeted.
