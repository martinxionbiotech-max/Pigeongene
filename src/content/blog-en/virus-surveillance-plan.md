Racing pigeons compete frequently and gather in large numbers at basketing, so the risk of virus transmission is high. Relying on a "treat after signs appear" approach is often too late. **Setting up a scientific virus surveillance plan** for proactive control is what separates a professional loft from an ordinary one.

## Why Do Virus Surveillance

The value of virus surveillance lies in:

- **Early detection**: detecting a pathogen before clinical signs appear, so you can act during the golden window for control;
- **Preventing spread**: identifying silently infected pigeons in good time and avoiding transmission through the flock;
- **Supporting decisions**: providing evidence for vaccination, quarantine of newly introduced birds and racing schedules;
- **Lowering costs**: proactive control costs far less than the losses after an outbreak.

## Designing a Surveillance Plan

### 1. Sampling Frequency

| Surveillance scenario | Suggested frequency |
|---|---|
| Pre-race screening | 1-2 weeks before each basketing |
| Quarantine of introduced birds | Every batch of newly introduced racing pigeons |
| Routine surveillance | Once per quarter |
| When an outbreak is nearby | Increase to once per month |

### 2. Test Panels

Matching risk against budget, tiered testing is recommended:

- **Basic panel**: Newcastle disease virus (PMV), adenovirus, Salmonella;
- **Extended panel**: pigeon circovirus, herpesvirus, Trichomonas, Candida albicans;
- **Comprehensive panel**: full coverage of 12 pathogens (including Mycoplasma, Chlamydia psittaci, rotavirus, polyomavirus and others).

### 3. Key Sampling Points

- **Pooled sampling**: within one loft you may sample several representative individuals, or collect group pooled samples (such as environmental swabs or drinker swabs);
- **Clear labelling**: mark every sample with the pigeon ring number, sampling date and loft number;
- **Prompt submission**: ship samples as soon as possible after collection and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

## Managing Results

Keep a file of test results so that you can:

- **Document everything**: archive every test report to build a health record;
- **Analyse trends**: compare results across rounds of testing to spot changes in underlying risk;
- **Handle positives**: on a positive result, isolate, re-test and disinfect immediately, and if necessary screen the whole flock.

## Environmental Monitoring as a Supplement

Besides testing individual birds, environmental monitoring matters just as much. Collecting environmental swabs regularly from loft floors, drinkers and feed troughs can assess the pathogen load in the environment and make up for gaps that individual sampling may leave.

## A Real Case: How a Carrier Pigeon Without Signs Was Found

During routine pre-race screening at one loft, a racing pigeon that looked normal and was eating actively tested positive for pigeon circovirus. The owner initially thought "no signs means nothing to worry about", but this virus can cause immunosuppression — a later Newcastle disease antibody test showed the bird's antibody level was clearly below the loft average. In the end the loft isolated the bird for observation and strengthened flock immunity and environmental disinfection, and no flock-wide respiratory problems occurred again that season.

The case makes two points: **having no signs does not mean not carrying the pathogen**; and **the value of surveillance is finding the risks you cannot see**, rather than only confirming a diagnosis in pigeons that are already ill.
